ABSTRACT
We analyze whether significant AI automation could accelerate economic growth by about an order of magnitude, similarly to how the Industrial Revolution accelerated global growth. We identify three primary drivers for such growth: 1) the scalability of an AI labor force restoring a regime of increasing returns to scale, 2) the rapid expansion of an AI labor force, and 3) the dramatic increase in output from rapid automation occurring over a brief period. Against this backdrop, we evaluate nine counterarguments, including regulatory hurdles, production bottlenecks, alignment issues, and the pace of automation. We argue that these counterarguments do not decisively rule out explosive growth. We conclude that AI systems capable of broadly substituting for human labor could plausibly lead to economic growth accelerating by an order of magnitude. However, high confidence in this outcome is unwarranted, given current uncertainties about the intensity of regulatory responses to AI, potential production bottlenecks from hard-to-quickly-accumulate inputs such as land, energy, and capital, the economic value of superhuman abilities, and the rate at which AI automation could occur.

1 INTRODUCTION
There has been growing interest in the possibility that advanced AI systems could drive explosive economic growth, meaning growth an order of magnitude faster than current rates. [Reviews: Trammell & Korinek 2020; Aghion et al. 2018 (Baumol effects: growth constrained by essential tasks resistant to productivity improvement); Hanson 2001 (post machine-intelligence growth ~40%/yr); Davidson 2021 (30% probability of explosive growth this century conditional on advanced AI).]

Overall, our assessment highlights four themes:
- Growth theory models generally predict explosive growth: "Standard models of economic growth consistently predict explosive growth when AI can effectively substitute for human labor across a majority or all economic tasks. This prediction holds true across a wide range of model types, including semi-endogenous and exogenous growth models, those with increasing or constant returns to scale, and models that incorporate investment delays."
- Regulation could restrict AI-driven growth... "such paths generally require lasting global coordination and potentially exerting control over many distributed actors, which might be infeasible given both the strengths of relevant incentives to develop and deploy advanced AI and the falling costs of AI training."
- Many arguments against explosive growth lack quantitative specificity or are otherwise weak.
- It is difficult to rule out explosive growth from AI, but that this should happen is far from certain: "We think that the odds of widespread automation and subsequent explosive growth by the end of this century are about even."

Definition: "explosive growth" = growth an order of magnitude greater than what is typical in today's frontier economies; annual real gross world product (GWP) exceeding 130% of its maximum value over all previous years.

2 ARGUMENTS IN FAVOR
"We present three arguments supporting the possibility of explosive growth from AI, all based on the concept that AI could serve as an accumulable substitute for human labor."

2.1 Increasing returns to scale in production gives rise to explosive growth
Standard R&D-based growth models with increasing returns to scale predict super-exponential growth when AI can effectively substitute for human labor. Y(A,K) = A * K^beta, with idea production (1/A)(dA/dt) ~ A^(-phi) I_A^lambda. As long as lambda/phi + beta > 1, the economy exhibits increasing returns and grows hyperbolically. Bloom et al. 2020 estimates lambda/phi ~ 0.32 for US TFP; hyperbolic growth occurs with beta as low as 0.68. "R&D-based growth models, and in particular, the semi-endogenous version, offer adequate explanations of recent and distant economic history... the fact that it robustly predicts explosive growth from AI that suitably substitutes for human labor should be considered a relatively strong argument."

2.2 The stock of digital workers could grow fast
Even a simple exogenous growth model (Y = A L^alpha K^(1-alpha), with L including digital workers accumulated by investment) predicts explosive growth if the cost of running an AI that substitutes for a human worker (c-bar, $/worker) is sufficiently low: c-bar <= s^(10/7) * $150,000/worker. Current ML hardware (~2e18 FLOP/($.year)) and Carlsmith's 1e15 FLOP/s brain estimate imply c-bar ~ $15,000/worker, consistent with explosive growth if savings rates s >= 0.2 — "in line with saving rates that have been historically observed in Western countries." [Also noted: AI-driven growth could raise savings, "given that AI could increase the productivity of capital investments, and result in concentrating wealth to those with a high propensity to save (Trammell and Korinek 2020), though it could also lower savings due to consumption smoothing considerations."] Caveats: higher-end brain-compute estimates make explosive growth unlikely at current prices; but hardware prices halve roughly every 2.5 years; AI systems can be copied, amortizing training costs, and "software progress on AI capabilities might not stop at human levels."

2.3 AI automation could have massive transitory effects
Even absent a permanent growth effect, a large level effect concentrated in time could clear the explosive-growth bar. In a CES task model Y = A (integral of I_i^rho di)^(1/rho) with rho<0 (tasks complements), automating a fraction 1-f of tasks multiplies output by f^((1-rho)/rho). "If f=0.1 and rho=-1, then the proportional increase in output is 100-fold. Such a large level-effect is likely to produce explosive growth unless it is spread out over a time frame much longer than a decade." Parameter values that would block massive increases require rho < -2 (elasticity of substitution < 1/3), "below the standard range that is considered plausible."

3 ARGUMENTS AGAINST (with authors' verdicts)
3.1 Regulations can slow down the economic impact of AI — plausible but: "we think our baseline scenario here for AI regulation should be more like nuclear arms control and less like the regulation of nuclear energy: coordination on nuclear arms control does happen, but it is quite imperfect." England's attempt to stop diffusion of Industrial Revolution technologies failed. Verdict: unlikely to block explosive growth.
3.2 Output is bottlenecked by other non-accumulable factors of production (land, energy, capital) — energy could expand ~3 OOMs (solar flux), built-up land ~2 OOMs; "if we can double the worldwide stock of physical capital at a rate of 30%/year, there is no reason to suppose that explosive growth would be prevented due to investment delays"; Chinese catch-up growth shows 10-15%/yr precedent. Verdict: unlikely (though "plausible on the outside view").
3.3 Technological progress and task automation by AI will be slow — requires wide/fat-tailed distribution of automation difficulty; inside-view investigations don't support >10 OOM gaps; with complementary tasks, "the final tasks to get automated remove the final bottlenecks in production," so a constant rate of automation produces back-loaded, eventually explosive growth. Verdict: unlikely, but "internally coherent and more compelling compared to most of the other objections."
3.4 Alignment difficulties could reduce the economic impact of AI — formally equivalent to limiting the automatable fraction of tasks; "most likely not going to block explosive growth, but its influence cannot be ruled out, especially in worlds where sigma turns out to be smaller than we expect." Verdict: unlikely.
3.5 R&D may be harder than expected — even with exogenous technology and diminishing returns, digital-worker accumulation can produce explosive growth. Verdict: very unlikely.
3.6 AI automation will fail to show up in the productivity statistics — GDP estimates in G7 are historically unbiased; free-goods mismeasurement adds only tens of basis points; "at some level, we do not care about productivity statistics in any fundamental sense." Verdict: unlikely.
3.7 Human preferences for human-produced goods will bottleneck growth — chess-player example; but "Even under the pessimistic assumptions of f=25% and rho=-2, AI that broadly substitutes for human workers should produce at least ~1 OOM increase in gross world product." More realistic f=5-10% and sigma=0.7 imply 3-4 OOM. "arguments based on intrinsic human preferences for some tasks being performed by humans seem like they would have made poor predictions if we had relied upon them in the past, and accordingly, we should be skeptical of them today as well." Verdict: very unlikely.
3.8 Previous technological revolutions did not lead to growth acceleration — sound as a prior for generic technologies, but "labor is unique in that it is an input that is both a key driver of economic production and growth and cannot be increased by reinvestment... the potential economic benefits of a technology that can turn labor into an accumulable input are enormous." Verdict: very unlikely.
3.9 Fundamental physical limits restrict economic growth — "we're simply too far from the relevant fundamental physical limits for the constraints imposed by them to be binding" (e.g., 1e40 FLOP/year feasible on Earth ~ 1e17 virtual workers). Verdict: very unlikely.

4 DISCUSSION
"The most significant finding is that economic growth models consistently predict explosive growth when AI can effectively replace human labor across most or all economic tasks. This prediction is robust across various types of models..."
"Having gone through the above arguments for and against explosive growth, we think that explosive growth by the end of this century is about as likely as not conditional [on] near-complete AI automation."
"Due to the numerous arguments against this conclusion... we think high confidence in explosive growth is unwarranted. However, we think confidence in explosive growth not occurring is also unwarranted, especially conditional on the arrival of AI capable of substituting for human workers."
The arguments are correlated (e.g., through the elasticity of substitution sigma and 'the overall level of problem-solving capabilities' of many human-level or superhuman intelligences). "After taking both the individual strength of the arguments and their overall correlation structure into account, we end up thinking that credences of less than 20% for explosive growth conditional on AI that can do most or all tasks in the economy are unreasonably low. We estimate P(explosive growth this century | AGI this century) about as likely as not."

Open questions include: competing theories of economic history; how value-add affects regulation strength; robotics costs; whether early alignment failures make deployment unprofitable; other bottleneck factors; the economic value of superhuman intelligence.
