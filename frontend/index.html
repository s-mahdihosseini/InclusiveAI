How will AI shape the distribution of prosperity?

AI could raise wages across the economy and rebuild the middle class — or concentrate enormous gains among a few firms, asset owners, and top earners. Which way it goes depends on a small set of economic mechanisms. This site turns each mechanism into an interactive general-equilibrium model you can explore under your own assumptions, or under scenarios distilled from what leading economists, technologists, and forecasters actually predict.

Two forces decide what AI does to the wage distribution

Expert occupations pay well partly because expertise is scarce: becoming a doctor, engineer, or lawyer takes years of training. Generative AI changes that in two opposing ways.

FORCE 1 · PUSHES TOP WAGES UP

Productivity gains

AI makes workers better at the jobs they already do — drafting contracts, reading scans, writing code. Because these gains land unevenly across occupations, the exposed (often high-paid) occupations produce more value per worker, pushing their wages up… though in general equilibrium, extra output also pushes the price of their services down.

FORCE 2 · PULLS TOP WAGES DOWN

Entry-barrier erosion

AI also shortens the road into expert work: with an AI assistant, tasks that took ten years of training become feasible after two. As barriers fall, more workers can enter high-paid occupations, expanding supply and competing away the scarcity rents that expertise used to command.

Which force wins? Productivity gains tend to widen the wage distribution; barrier erosion compresses it. The outcome depends on how strong each channel is and how substitutable occupations are (σ). Set those three dials below — or load an expert scenario — and the model solves exact general-equilibrium wages for 885 occupations.

A general-purpose technology (like computers, or AI) gets cheap. Which occupations gain? An occupation wins the direct race when the demand elasticity for its output (ε) exceeds its labor–GPT substitution elasticity (σ) — cheaper technology expands its market faster than it replaces its workers. On top of that, Engel forces shift spending toward income-elastic services as the economy grows, worker mobility spreads gains and losses, and household heterogeneity feeds the income distribution back into demand. AI gets better — who captures the gains, and how fast?

When AI productivity jumps, the industry wants to scale up. But the AI industry has convex supply curves: expanding output gets disproportionately expensive, because it depends on things that are hard to scale — power, data centers, chips, proprietary data — and on compute that takes time to build. Convexity is what turns productivity gains into rents instead of lower prices. Two parameters govern it all: how severe the decreasing returns are, and how costly it is to build fast.

FORCE 1 · CONVEX SUPPLY

The AI industry is hard to scale

Expanding AI output isn't like printing software copies: it needs power interconnections, data centers, chip supply, proprietary data, and organizational capability — factors that get disproportionately expensive to add the more you add. That convex supply curve means a productivity breakthrough does not fully pass through into cheaper AI: part of the gain is retained as rents to whoever owns the hard-to-replicate factors. The supply convexity slider sets how severe these decreasing returns are.

FORCE 2 · TIME TO BUILD

Compute scarcity is temporary — but how temporary?

Even the parts that can scale take time: a permanent AI breakthrough hits a predetermined compute stock. On impact, the scarcity shows up as a spike in compute rental prices and in the value of installed compute (Tobin's q). Investment then gradually builds the stock and competes the scarcity away. The adjustment cost φ doesn't change the destination — it sets how long the bottleneck rents last before productivity fully reaches prices.

The punchline: convex supply curves turn productivity gains into rents — permanently for the hard-to-replicate factors, temporarily for installed compute. How big and how persistent those rents are is what the two sliders control.

The models behind the sliders

Every result on this site is solved from an explicit general-equilibrium model. This page states each model: the setup, the AI shock, the equilibrium conditions, and exactly which parameter each slider moves.

▸ Expertise & Entry Barriers  — Hosseini & Lichtinger (2026), 885 O*NET occupations
Setup: occupations gated by training

Occupations \(o = 1,\dots,J\) are ranked by their entry barrier: the training time \(R_o\) needed to work in them. A worker can enter occupation \(o\) only if their (heterogeneous) training capacity clears the barrier, so the eligible share for each occupation is \(ps_o = 1 - F(R_o)\), where \(F\) is the population distribution of training capacity. Eligibility is hierarchical: anyone who clears a high barrier also clears all lower ones.

Labor supply: choice among feasible occupations

Workers draw Type-I extreme-value taste shocks with scale \(\tau\) and choose the best feasible occupation. A worker whose highest feasible occupation is \(k\) chooses \(o \le k\) with logit probability, so employment is

$$L_o \;=\; \bar L \sum_{k \ge o} \mu_k \, \frac{w_o^{1/\tau}}{\sum_{j \le k} w_j^{1/\tau}}, \qquad \mu_k = F(R_{k+1}) - F(R_k),$$

where \(\mu_k\) is the mass of workers whose highest feasible occupation is \(k\).

Labor demand: CES across occupations

Final output aggregates occupational labor with elasticity of substitution \(\sigma\), giving inverse demand

$$w_o \;=\; \psi_o\, A_o^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}}\, L_o^{-1/\sigma},$$

with \(\psi_o\) calibrated so the baseline matches observed wages and employment, and \(A_o\) an occupation-specific productivity shifter.

The AI shock: two channels

Scarcity channel. AI shortens retraining: barriers fall from \(R_o\) to \(R_o^{AI}\), raising eligibility \(ps_o\) most in high-barrier occupations. Productivity channel. AI raises occupational productivity by \(\pi_o\) (\(A_o = e^{\pi_o}\)). Both \(R_o^{AI}\) and \(\pi_o\) are measured at the occupation level with LLM task-based ratings.

Partial equilibrium (holding others' wages fixed)
$$\Delta \ln w_o^{PE} \;=\; -\frac{1}{\sigma}\,\Delta \ln ps_o \;+\; \frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}\,\pi_o.$$

More eligibility lowers the wage (scarcity rents erode); productivity raises it when \(\sigma > 1\).

General equilibrium

Wages solve the fixed point (all 885 occupations simultaneously):

$$w \;=\; \psi \cdot e^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}\pi} \cdot L(w;\,ps)^{-1/\sigma},$$

solved by damped iteration to machine precision. \(\tau\) is calibrated (\(\hat\tau \approx 0.72\)) so the model's baseline employment shares match the data.

What the sliders move

σ — the CES elasticity above. Scarcity intensity \(s\): \(F^{s} = F_0 + s\,(F_{AI} - F_0)\), interpolating between no barrier change (\(s=0\)) and the estimated AI effect (\(s=1\)). Productivity intensity \(p\): scales \(\pi_o\) to \(p \cdot \pi_o\).

▸ Demand Structure & GPT Incidence  — 4 occupation-goods + GPT sector, nonhomothetic demand, worker mobility
Setup: production with a general-purpose technology input

Each occupation \(i \in \{\text{manual services, production, clerical, professional}\}\) produces its own good using labor and GPT services \(X_i\):

$$Y_i = A_i\Big[(1-\alpha_i)L_i^{\rho_i} + \alpha_i X_i^{\rho_i}\Big]^{1/\rho_i}, \qquad \rho_i = \tfrac{\sigma_i - 1}{\sigma_i},$$

where \(\sigma_i\) is the labor–GPT substitution elasticity and \(\alpha_i\) is set to match the occupation's baseline GPT cost share \(b_i\) (its exposure). The GPT sector produces \(Y_g = A_g L_g\) with wage numeraire \(w_g = 1\), so the GPT price is \(p_g = 1/A_g\).

Households: nonhomothetic demand

Preferences are implicitly additive (Hanoch 1975): the expenditure function solves

$$\sum_j \Omega_j\, (p_j/E)^{1-\varepsilon_j}\, U^{\xi_j (1-\varepsilon_j)} = 1,$$

which delivers a good-specific price elasticity \(\varepsilon_j\) and income-elasticity parameter \(\xi_j\) (Engel forces). Budget shares follow from Shephard's lemma. In the heterogeneous version, each education group \(m \in \{\text{low, mid, college}\}\) is its own household with income \(I_m = \sum_i w_i L_{mi}\), so the income distribution feeds back into demand.

Workers: Roy/logit mobility
$$\pi_{mi} = \frac{T_{mi}\, w_i^{\kappa_m}}{\sum_j T_{mj}\, w_j^{\kappa_m}}, \qquad L_i = \sum_m N_m \pi_{mi},$$

with mobility elasticities \(\kappa_m\); \(\kappa = 0\) is the immobile benchmark.

The AI shock and equilibrium

GPT productivity \(A_g\) rises along a path from 1 to its final value. At each point, wages clear all four goods markets, \(C_i(p, I) = Y_i\), with the GPT market clearing by Walras' law. The exact nonlinear system is solved by Newton's method, warm-started along the path.

The central incidence result

Cheaper GPT services help occupation \(i\) when the demand elasticity for its output exceeds its substitution elasticity, \(\varepsilon_i > \sigma_i\): the market expands faster than labor is displaced. On top of this race come income effects (via \(\xi_i\)), mobility, and the distributional demand feedback.

What the sliders move

A_g — final GPT productivity multiplier. Mobility — scales \(\kappa_m\). Exposure — scales baseline GPT cost shares \(b_i\). Demand-elasticity dispersion — compresses/amplifies the spread of \(\varepsilon_i\) around its mean (0 = one common elasticity, the CES benchmark). Nonhomotheticity — scales \(\xi_i - 1\) (0 = homothetic). σ scale — scales \(\ln \sigma_i\). Households — pooled representative household vs. one household per education group.

▸ Compute Bottlenecks  — Hosseini & Lichtinger (2026), closed-form q-theory of AI supply
Setup: final production and AI supply

Final output combines general labor \(L_1\) (fixed) and AI services \(X\) with elasticity \(\sigma\):

$$Y = \Big[\alpha^{1/\sigma} L_1^{\rho} + (1-\alpha)^{1/\sigma} X^{\rho}\Big]^{1/\rho}, \qquad \frac{d\ln p_X}{d\ln X} = -\frac{s_1}{\sigma} \equiv -\theta,$$

where \(s_1\) is labor's revenue share. The AI sector produces

$$X_t = A_t\, K_t^{\gamma} L_{2t}^{\beta} Z^{1-\beta-\gamma},$$

with compute \(K\), variable labor \(L_2\) (supply elasticity \(\eta\)), and a hard-to-scale fixed factor \(Z\) (data, sites, organization). The fixed-factor share \(1-\beta-\gamma\) is the supply convexity slider: it is why the industry's supply curve is convex and why rents exist, \(\Pi_Z = (1-\beta-\gamma)\, p_X X\).

Static pass-through of an AI productivity shock

With \(\theta = s_1/\sigma\), \(D = 1 + \eta(1-\beta+\beta\theta)\) and \(H = (1+\eta)/D\):

$$\frac{d\ln X}{d\ln A} = H, \qquad \frac{d\ln p_X}{d\ln A} = -\theta H, \qquad \frac{d\ln \Pi_Z}{d\ln A} = (1-\theta) H.$$
Dynamics: compute takes time to build

Compute accumulates with quadratic installation costs \(\Phi = \tfrac{\phi}{2}\big(\tfrac{I}{K}-\delta\big)^2 K\), giving the q-theory investment rule and Euler equation

$$q_t = 1 + \phi\,(i_t - \delta), \qquad q_t = \mathfrak b\,\mathbb E_t\Big[r^K_{t+1} + (1-\delta) q_{t+1} + \tfrac{\phi}{2}(i_{t+1}^2 - \delta^2)\Big].$$
Closed-form transition after a permanent shock

With \(B = (1-\theta)H\) and \(a = 1-\gamma B\), the saddle path has stable root

$$\lambda = \tfrac{1}{2}\Big[T - \sqrt{T^2 - 4/\mathfrak b}\Big], \qquad T = 1 + \tfrac{1}{\mathfrak b} + \tfrac{r^K_\star\, a}{\phi},$$
$$\hat K_t = \frac{B}{a}\big(1-\lambda^t\big), \qquad \hat r^K_t = B\,\lambda^t, \qquad \hat q_t = \phi\, \hat K_\infty (1-\lambda)\lambda^t.$$

Higher \(\phi\) raises \(\lambda\): it changes how long compute scarcity (and its temporary rents) lasts — not the impact response, and not the long run.

What the sliders move

Supply convexity \(\zeta = 1-\beta-\gamma\), with the variable-input share split equally between \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\). Time to build \(\phi\). Fixed calibration: \(\sigma = 2\), \(s_X = 0.5\), \(\eta = 1\), \(\mathfrak b = 0.99\), \(\delta = 0.025\) (quarterly), 1% permanent shock.

About InclusiveAI

InclusiveAI is the interactive companion to AI and the Distribution of Prosperity: A Unified Framework for Understanding How Technology Shapes Inequality by Guy Lichtinger and Seyed M. Hosseini (Harvard University, 2026). The paper organizes the mechanisms through which AI affects inequality into three stages — direct effects on production, the division of productivity gains, and general-equilibrium demand effects — and identifies the factors that determine each mechanism's direction and magnitude.

Authors: Guy Lichtinger · Seyed M. Hosseini.

Each tab on this site wraps a quantitative general-equilibrium model of one mechanism. Move the parameters yourself, or load scenarios distilled from a growing corpus of expert views (economists, lab leaders, forecasters, skeptics) mapped to model parameters through a fixed extraction rubric.
