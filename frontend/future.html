What does your view of AI imply for inequality?

Answer four questions about who AI replaces, whom it helps, who owns it, and how much power employers have. Each answer sets parameters of a calibrated general-equilibrium model with risky capital and an unequal wealth distribution. The model then reports what happens to labor income, capital income, and inequality in the long run.

Model: an AI extension of Moll, Rachel & Restrepo (2022), “Uneven Growth,” with occupation-level AI exposure and wage markdowns. Steady-state comparisons, not forecasts.

How the model works

Production. Ten occupation groups (equal-employment deciles of the wage distribution) each perform a range of tasks. AI can automate tasks, moving them from workers to capital, or augment the tasks that remain with workers, raising their productivity. Which occupations are exposed comes from task-level AI-exposure data (Eloundou et al.); your answers to questions 1–2 can tilt that exposure toward low- or high-wage jobs while keeping its average size fixed.

Wages and profits. Employers pay workers a markdown below their marginal product, calibrated to Seegmiller's estimates by skill (8–13 percent before AI). The markdown is a profit for the firm. Question 4 scales markdowns for low-wage and high-wage occupations.

Who owns capital. Capital, AI, and business profits are held as risky equity. Only a fraction of households can hold it (6.6 percent in the calibration); everyone else saves in safe bonds. Investors lever up when the return on risky capital is high, which thickens the top of the wealth distribution. Question 3 sets this fraction.

What is held fixed. The overall size of the AI shock (adjustable under “Advanced”), preferences, and the asset-market parameters from Moll, Rachel & Restrepo. Every result compares two balanced-growth steady states, so it describes the long run, not the transition.
